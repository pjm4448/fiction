# Save the Cat! Writes a Novel (Epic Fantasy Example) 

In **Save the Cat! Writes a Novel** by Jessica Brody, fantasy stories don't have their own dedicated "genre" slot among the 10 story genres she adapts from Blake Snyder. Fantasy (along with sci-fi, historical, etc.) is a **surface genre** (setting/world-building), while the book's 10 genres are deeper **story types** focused on the core emotional journey, conflict, and theme.

Many epic/high fantasy novels fit best into the **Superhero** genre (a special/unique hero discovers their power/destiny, faces overwhelming odds, learns to wield it responsibly, and saves the world). Others lean toward **Golden Fleece** (quest/journey stories like classic fantasy adventures), **Out of the Bottle** (magic/wish-granting tales with rules and consequences), or even **Dude with a Problem** (ordinary person thrust into survival against dark forces).

The **15-beat structure** remains the same across all genres, but Brody tailors advice for each of the 10 story types with genre-specific examples, pitfalls, and tweaks (e.g., how stakes escalate differently in Superhero vs. Rites of Passage).

Here's a **generic example beat sheet** for a classic epic fantasy novel using the Save the Cat! structure. Imagine a young farmhand (or apprentice mage/soldier) who discovers they're the prophesied chosen one to defeat an ancient dark lord threatening the realm.

### Example Beat Sheet: Epic Fantasy (Superhero-style)

1. **Opening Image** (0–1%)  
   A quiet, mundane village scene: the hero tends crops or trains clumsily with a wooden sword under a vast, magical sky. We see a world of subtle wonder (floating lanterns, distant dragons in the clouds) but the hero feels ordinary and overlooked.

2. **Theme Stated** (≈5%)  
   An old mentor/elder says something like: "True power isn't born—it's chosen, and it demands sacrifice." (Theme: Power/responsibility requires personal cost/growth.)

3. **Set-Up** (1–10%)  
   Introduce the hero's flawed status quo: they're kind but insecure, dream of adventure but fear change. Show the world (kingdom in decline, whispers of returning darkness), family/friends, and the hero's "save the cat" moment (helping a villager or animal in need to make them likable).

4. **Catalyst** (≈10%)  
   The inciting incident: a dark rider attacks the village, or the hero finds a glowing artifact/awakens latent magic during a raid. Their life is irrevocably shattered—they're marked as the prophesied one.

5. **Debate** (10–20%)  
   The hero hesitates: "I'm no hero—I'm just a nobody." They try to run/hide, question the prophecy, or seek advice from skeptical allies. Internal conflict peaks before they commit.

6. **Break into Act Two** (≈20–25%)  
   Hero leaves home (crosses the threshold): accepts the call, sets off on the quest with a ragtag group (mentor, comic-relief sidekick, love interest/rival). The ordinary world is behind them; the fantastical adventure begins.

7. **B Story** (≈22%)  
   The emotional/romantic subplot: perhaps a bond with a fierce warrior companion or a mysterious elf who challenges the hero's views on power/trust. This relationship teaches the theme (e.g., "You can't wield power alone—you need others").

8. **Fun and Games** (20–50%)  
   The "promise of the premise"—the exciting fantasy stuff readers want! Training montages, exploring enchanted forests, battling minor monsters, gathering allies, discovering magic/rules of the world, narrow escapes, and rising victories. The hero starts believing in their destiny.

9. **Midpoint** (≈50%)  
   Stakes-raising false victory: hero wins a major battle, claims a powerful artifact, or publicly reveals themselves as the chosen one. Feels like they've "leveled up"—but it's a false high (e.g., the dark lord now knows exactly where they are).

10. **Bad Guys Close In** (50–75%)  
    Complications mount: betrayals (ally turns traitor), losses (mentor dies or is captured), internal doubts resurface, magic backfires, team fractures. External forces (armies, curses) tighten; hero's flaws (arrogance, fear) cause mistakes.

11. **All Is Lost** (≈75%)  
    Rock bottom: the hero fails catastrophically—perhaps a key ally dies, the artifact is destroyed/corrupted, or the dark lord overruns a major stronghold. "Whiff of death" (literal or symbolic). Everything seems hopeless.

12. **Dark Night of the Soul** (75–80%)  
    Hero despairs in isolation: questions the prophecy, mourns losses, confronts their deepest fear ("I'm not strong enough"). This is the emotional low point.

13. **Break into Act Three** (≈80%)  
    Epiphany! Tied to the B Story/theme: the hero realizes true power comes from sacrifice, unity, or self-belief (not raw strength). They rally with renewed purpose, often gaining unexpected help.

14. **Finale** (80–99%)  
    The big climax: hero storms the dark lord's fortress, assembles the team for a final stand, uses lessons learned (and magic/power correctly), confronts the antagonist in an epic battle. Subplots resolve (romance, side quests). Hero wins through growth, not just force.

15. **Final Image** (99–100%)  
    Mirror of the Opening Image: the hero stands in a restored/peaceful kingdom (or their village rebuilt), now confident and changed. The sky is brighter, magic feels balanced—they've become the hero they were destined to be.

This is flexible—percentages shift slightly in longer epic fantasies (e.g., more room in Fun and Games for world-building). For quest-heavy fantasy, lean Golden Fleece (road-trip structure with team growth). For magic-granting tales, use Out of the Bottle (wish → rules → lesson).

If you're outlining your own fantasy novel, start by deciding which of the 10 story genres fits your core conflict best, then plug in specifics. Jessica Brody's book has full example breakdowns for each of the 10 genres (with published novel references), so it's worth checking those for closer matches.
